Development Operations · agentic AI skill prototype

Protocol Eligibility Copilot

Eligibility criteria decide how fast a trial can enrol, how many patients fail screening, and how many protocol amendments follow. This prototype turns the criteria of a real protocol into structured, reviewable data: each criterion is categorised, its thresholds and washout windows are pulled out, and anything a feasibility reviewer should question is flagged. It then benchmarks the protocol against completed trials in the same indication and phase.

The point of the exercise is the method as much as the tool. I picked one Development Operations workflow, set a baseline, built the skill with frontier agentic tools, measured it against that baseline on quality, cost, latency and consistency, and wrote down what it would take to use it in a GxP setting.

1 · Background

Clinical trials and eligibility criteria, in plain words

Clinical trials. Before a new medicine or vaccine can be approved, a drug company (the sponsor) tests it on volunteers in clinical trials. A Phase 3 trial is the large, final stage, often with hundreds or thousands of participants at dozens of hospitals (sites) in many countries. It usually takes years and costs a great deal.

The protocol. Each trial follows a written rulebook called the protocol. It covers what is being tested, on whom, how and for how long. Sponsors publish a summary of every protocol on ClinicalTrials.gov, the public US registry used worldwide. This tool reads from it.

Eligibility criteria. One part of the protocol sets out who may take part. Inclusion criteria are what a volunteer must have, and exclusion criteria are what rules them out. A trial typically has 10 to 40 of them, written in dense medical language. Every extra restriction shrinks the pool of patients who qualify. That slows recruitment, raises the share of candidates who are screened and then turned away, and often forces costly protocol changes (amendments) later.

What the AI agent produces

It turns one block of medical text for each criterion into a structured row that a person can scan, sort and question. Examples from GSK protocols in this demo:

CategoryWhat kind of rule it is, such as age or weight, the disease itself, past treatments, lab results, other illnesses, pregnancy, or consent. Grouping shows at a glance where a protocol is most restrictive.
ThresholdThe number that decides eligibility, such as a BMI of at least 16 or a lung function reading below 70%.
Washout windowHow long a volunteer must wait after a previous treatment before joining, such as 90 days. Long washouts delay or block enrolment.
Feasibility flagsPrompts for a human to look again: a rule that adds screening work, relies on a doctor's judgement, is worded ambiguously, or is the kind of exclusion that FDA and oncology groups now encourage sponsors to relax unless it is scientifically needed, such as blanket exclusion of people with HIV.
Feasibility and the benchmarkFeasibility asks whether a trial can realistically recruit the patients it needs, on time and at the planned sites. The tool compares the protocol with the median figures of completed trials of the same disease and phase. The depemokimab COPD trial plans 1,196 participants, against a median of about 330 in comparable completed COPD Phase 3 trials. That gap is a useful question to ask early.
The reviewerThe output is a draft for a person on the study team, such as a feasibility or clinical operations lead. They check every row and correct anything wrong. The tool never decides whether any patient is eligible, and it only uses public registry text.

The goal

Give study teams a fast, consistent first pass that challenges eligibility criteria at the design stage, before sites see the protocol. That is the cheapest point to remove unnecessary restrictions, and it should mean faster recruitment and fewer amendments. The wider aim is a repeatable way to bring agentic AI into a regulated workflow:

  1. Pick one real workflow.
  2. Measure a simple baseline.
  3. Build the AI step against a fixed contract.
  4. Prove it beats the baseline on quality, cost, speed and consistency.
  5. Keep a human accountable for every output.

3 · Evaluation

How we know it works

The evaluation harness works like an exam with an answer key.

  1. The answer key. Before any AI was run, each of the 70 criteria from the 5 protocols was given its correct category by hand. A few criteria genuinely fit two categories, and for those a second answer is also accepted.
  2. Two candidates take the exam. The baseline is a simple program that matches keywords: if it sees "vaccine" it answers Prior therapy, and if it sees "history of" or "disease" it answers Comorbidity. It was written before the AI was tested and never adjusted to fit the answer key. The AI is Claude, given the same criteria and the written category definitions.
  3. Marking. Each answer is compared with the key.

Real examples where the two disagreed:

    Is it practical to use?

      Where the baseline goes wrong

      What about the flags?

      The category is only half of the output. For each criterion the AI also decides whether to raise flags that ask a reviewer to look again. We don't yet know whether the flags are right. The category has an answer key; the flags don't, because "does this criterion deserve a second look?" is a judgement call that needs clinical operations experts to answer.

      What we can measure now is consistency. It is a necessary condition, not proof of correctness. If the AI raises a flag on one run and not the next, or two models disagree about the same criterion, that flag can't be trusted yet.

      Agreement means: of all criteria where either model raised the flag, the share where both did.

      What this tells us

        How we would find out properly

        1. Tighten the definitions. For example, limit modernisation candidate to the exclusion types the guidance actually lists, and require a one-line reason for every flag.
        2. Build an answer key for flags. Two clinical operations reviewers label the same criteria independently. Check first that they agree with each other; if experts disagree, the flag itself needs redefining.
        3. Score the AI on two questions. Of the flags it raises, how many do experts agree with? That measures crying wolf. Of the criteria experts would flag, how many did it catch? That measures missed issues.
        4. Keep measuring in a pilot. Record whether reviewers accept or dismiss each flag. That gives a running, real-world score.
        4 · Agentic design

        Design choices, mapped to how agentic systems fail

        I built this as a constrained workflow rather than a free-roaming agent. Orchestration is deterministic and the model is called at one step, with a fixed contract. That makes it cheaper to validate and easier to explain to Quality, and it leaves room to add autonomy where it pays.

        ContextEach call gets one protocol: the study metadata, the deterministically split criteria with stable IDs, and the taxonomy definitions. No retrieval guesswork and no patient data. The prompt is generated from the same taxonomy object in steps.js that the baseline and the scorer use.
        MemoryStateless by design: nothing carries between protocols, so results are reproducible. The frozen CT.gov snapshot and saved model runs are the only stored data; the reviewer audit trail lives in the browser tab in this demo. Cross-study memory, such as a library of past amendments, is a next step with its own governance.
        Tool useTwo tools: get_study and find_comparators, both against the public ClinicalTrials.gov API v2. Tools are deterministic, so their outputs can be tested like ordinary code.
        OrchestrationrunPipeline() in orchestrator.js: fetch → split → classify → benchmark → human review. Fixed order, with fallbacks at each step: the rule baseline if no model is available, and the frozen snapshot if the registry is unreachable.
        EvaluationA reference set, a pre-registered baseline, and four metrics: accuracy, cost, latency and run-to-run agreement. The harness (eval/run_eval.mjs) reruns end to end with one command, so a model or prompt change gets re-scored before it ships.
        ReliabilityOutputs are schema-checked and fail closed: a missing or malformed answer becomes NEEDS_REVIEW and is never silently defaulted. Two runs per protocol measure consistency. A named human signs off every output.
        5 · Clinical context

        Where this sits in clinical development

        Eligibility criteria are written at protocol design but their cost lands later, in feasibility, screen failure, recruitment timelines and amendments. ICH E8(R1) asks sponsors to design quality in by focusing on factors critical to quality. FDA's 2020 guidance on eligibility criteria and the ASCO–Friends of Cancer Research recommendations push back on exclusions that are not scientifically necessary. This skill gives study teams that challenge at design time, before sites see the protocol.

        6 · Recorded vs live

        What gets saved, and what doesn't

        The evaluation results are static, captured once. All 30 model runs (3 models × 2 runs × 5 protocols) were made on 1 Oct 2026 by running the evaluation harness locally. They were committed to the repository by hand, one file per call in eval/runs/, holding the model's raw answer, cost, time and token counts. No automation adds or changes them: there is no CI job, schedule or bot.

        This page never saves anything. It is a static site, so it can read files from the repository but cannot write to it.

        Classifier on this pageWhat happensSaved?
        Rule baselineKeyword rules run in your browserNo
        Claude Haiku / Sonnet / Opus · recordedShows the stored answers from those 30 runsNo; read only
        Claude Sonnet 5.5 · liveCalls Claude from your browser with your own API keyNo; gone when you reload

        The live ClinicalTrials.gov lookups and the reviewer audit trail also exist only in your browser tab. The audit trail illustrates the idea. A real deployment in a regulated (GxP) setting would store each reviewer decision permanently, with the reviewer's name and a timestamp.

        New results enter the repository only on purpose. Someone re-runs the evaluation locally, checks the new scores, and commits them, so the history shows when the numbers changed and why. A fixed test set with saved answers keeps every score reproducible: anyone can re-score the same answers with a new metric without calling the models again.

        7 · How it was built

        Built in a day, with the evidence attached

        Built in one day with Claude Code as the agentic development environment. The pieces: steps.js holds the step implementations: splitter, baseline, taxonomy, prompt, parser, scorer and the two registry tools. orchestrator.js holds the flow, runPipeline(), which this page and the evaluation harness both call, so what is evaluated is exactly what runs here. models.js holds the model adapter that live mode passes into the pipeline. skills/eligibility-criteria-structurer/SKILL.md packages the step as a reusable agent skill, with its standards. eval/ holds the frozen protocols, reference labels, raw model runs and the harness.

        Limitations. The reference set is small (70 criteria), and the labels were drafted with AI assistance before human review, which can favour model-style labelling. The categories are deliberately coarse, so the task saturates. The feasibility flags are checked only for consistency, not correctness, and the thresholds are not yet scored. Costs come from Claude Code's harness at list price, so they are an upper bound on a direct API call. Manual review time has not been measured yet; a time-and-motion baseline with study teams is the next step before any value claim on cycle time.

        Prototype for discussion. Uses only public registry data from ClinicalTrials.gov and makes no eligibility decision about any patient. Not affiliated with or endorsed by GSK.
